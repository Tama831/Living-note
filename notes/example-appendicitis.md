## 📌 Five-line summary
<!-- LN:SUMMARY:START -->
- Appendicitis is the most common cause of a surgical acute abdomen. Lifetime risk is 6-9%, with a peak at ages 10-19. Its pathogenesis can be framed as a "nutrition-microbiome-genetic axis," and uncomplicated and complicated appendicitis may be "two different diseases" with distinct cytokine profiles (Ryoo / Han 2026). In pediatric complicated appendicitis, the most common causative organism is E. coli, although the certainty of this evidence is low (Almeida-Gomes 2026)
- Diagnosis is converging on "**don't decide on a single test, and don't decide at a single time point**": Alvarado cannot be used alone, the continuous pARC outperforms fixed additive-point scores, and early equivocal cases should be **observed and re-measured** (AUROC 0.629 for a single AAS → 0.932 for ∆AAS). Furthermore, **scores do not transport across populations**: AIR, which performs well on specificity in children, reaches only AUC 0.752 in pregnant women (Tchouala Tchakoute / Lastunen / Tavakoli 2026)
- Among imaging options, in-hospital CT is the most accurate, MRI is the choice in pregnancy (sensitivity 0.95 / specificity 0.97), and POCUS is for rule-in only. The high AUCs reported for AI/ML **reflect "methodological fragility rather than clinical readiness"**, because calibration, external validation and clinical utility are not reported (Bašković & Pogorelić 2026). The focus is moving from an accuracy race to triage in primary care (Hogervorst 2026, NCT06762275)
- For non-perforated appendicitis, surgery vs antibiotics is a matter of shared decision-making, but **the picture changes over time**. Beyond 2 years of follow-up, NOM failure is 38.9% and 36.3% of patients eventually undergo appendectomy, while the cost advantage of €1535 is maintained over the long term (Kahana 2026). On the surgical side, laparoscopy remains the gold standard, and attention is turning to standardizing technical details (WSES 2025-aligned, Štefanová 2026)
- Complicated appendicitis calls for an **individualized step-up approach** (IV antibiotics + percutaneous drainage → resection). The decision axis for interval appendectomy is shifting toward "age-linked neoplasm risk" (post-appendicitis colorectal neoplasia in 0.5-34.6% of patients aged ≥40). The library has grown from 3 to 43 papers, a pace that calls for a "living review" like the living evidence map (Kleindienst 2026)

## 🧵 Cross-cutting synthesis
<!-- LN:SYNTHESIS:START -->
### Diagnosis
The AHRQ systematic review of diagnostic accuracy for right lower quadrant pain (Dahabreh 2015, more than 1000 studies) concluded that **"symptoms, physical signs and blood tests are not sufficient on their own; confirmation comes from imaging."** That conclusion is still the backbone. CT has sensitivity 0.96-1.0 and specificity 0.91-1.0 and is the most accurate test in every population studied. In pregnant women, MRI is the main alternative (Danawar 2026). An updated meta-analysis of 34 studies (Habiro Alves 2026) gave this position **quantitative support: sensitivity 0.95, specificity 0.97 (AUC 0.961)**. MRI is now recommended as "the standard second-line test after an inconclusive ultrasound." The observation that "US is operator-dependent" has been quantified in the POCUS era. POCUS performed by emergency physicians has sensitivity 0.78 and specificity 0.89, so **a positive scan can rule in, but a negative scan cannot rule out**, and accuracy depends on whether the operator has fellowship training (Kamal 2026, meta-analysis of 15 studies).

The role of clinical scores has also been clarified: they have moved from "adjunct" to **"never to be used alone; one component of a stepwise multimodal pathway."** A review comparing Alvarado with alternative tools in children (Tchouala Tchakoute 2026, literature from 2016-2026) found that Alvarado has good sensitivity but **specificity of only 57-76% at a cutoff of ≥7**, so it cannot be used alone. Among pediatric-specific scores, **PAS is better for sensitivity and AIR for specificity, and AIR also discriminates complicated disease such as gangrene, phlegmon and perforation well**. In addition, **a continuous risk model (pARC) outperforms fixed additive-point scores in pediatric cohorts**, which moves scoring from "adding up points" to "estimating probability." The review concludes that the best approach adds CRP, the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and selective ultrasound to the score, and reserves CT for indeterminate cases.

However, **score performance does not transport across populations**. AIR was valued for its specificity in children. The first diagnostic accuracy meta-analysis of AIR in pregnant women (Tavakoli 2026, 5 studies, bivariate random-effects model) found only moderate performance: **pooled sensitivity 66.7% (95%CI 44.2-83.5), specificity 75.5% (40.0-93.5) and AUC 0.752**, with a false-positive rate of 24.5%. Heterogeneity was large (I² 75.6%), and **the choice of cutoff** was one of its sources. The cutoff shifts specificity significantly but not sensitivity. Within a single study, raising the cutoff from ≥4 to ≥9 lowered sensitivity from 99.1% to 36.8% and raised specificity from 42.4% to 97.0%. Pregnancy changes the white cell count and CRP physiologically, so it is not surprising that inflammation-based scores are unstable. The authors conclude that AIR should be **an adjunct to clinical assessment, not a standalone diagnostic test**, and call for pregnancy-specific cutoffs to be set in prospective multicenter studies. These numbers support the upstream pathway in pregnancy of "narrow down with a score, decide with MRI." That said, the analysis includes only 5 studies and the confidence intervals are wide.

The 2026-09 arrival added **the dimension of "time"** to this move away from standalone scores. Lastunen 2026 is a secondary analysis of the DIAMOND trial's observation arm (83 cases) that included only adults with early, hard-to-judge presentations (early equivocal). **A single Adult Appendicitis Score (AAS) at presentation had an AUROC of 0.629 (95%CI 0.498-0.760), which is barely usable.** After a short period of observation, the **re-scored final AAS reached 0.936 (0.886-0.987), and the change in score during observation (∆AAS) reached 0.932 (0.868-0.996)**. ∆CRP was intermediate at 0.796. Using the thresholds given in the abstract (low ≤ -2 / intermediate -1 to 0 / high ≥ 1), **the negative predictive value in the low-probability group was 97% and the positive predictive value in the high-probability group was 94%**. So for early equivocal abdominal pain, **"measuring again and looking at the difference"** sometimes works better than adding tests. It is also a strategy for **reducing the overdiagnosis and radiation exposure** that come with early CT. However, this is a secondary analysis of a single trial with n=83. The thresholds were derived from an ROC analysis of the same data (no external validation), and the optimal observation time is not reported. Scores have now moved in two steps: from "adding up points" to "estimating probability," and then to "**a function of time**." This follows the same design philosophy as "don't decide on a single test," the principle of the primary-care strategy described below.

New biomarkers are also emerging. IL-6, considered promising in children (Tan & Bo 2026), is now supported by prospective adult data: in an analysis of 48 cytokines, complicated appendicitis had significantly higher IL-6, HGF and MCP-1, and IL-6 alone predicted complicated disease with an AUC of 0.785 (Han 2026). AI models have followed (AUC 0.85-0.96). CT-based deep learning is the most accurate, but most studies are retrospective and single-center, and external validation is lacking (Ismayilzada 2026). A critical methodological review has now questioned these "high AUCs" themselves (Bašković & Pogorelić 2026). It covers clinical scores, machine learning, deep learning and large language models for pediatric appendicitis across **the whole care pathway: binary diagnosis, severity stratification, prediction of NOM response, imaging stewardship and postoperative course**. Appraised against the TRIPOD+AI / PROBAST+AI criteria, **reported performance focuses on discrimination, while calibration, clinical utility, external validation, fairness and reproducibility are reported inconsistently or not at all**. The authors state that "the recurring pattern of very high accuracy reflects **methodological fragility** more than clinical readiness," and they provide a checklist for critically reading prediction studies. As scores move further toward "estimating probability," the key question becomes less about ranking ability (AUC) and more about **whether the predicted probabilities are accurate (calibration)**. That is exactly where the evidence is missing, and the external validation of pARC and ∆AAS needs the same yardstick.

This whole accuracy debate, however, has assumed **patients who have already reached the hospital or ER.** The 2026-08 arrival moves the discussion upstream, **to the entry point of primary care**. A hybrid type 1 (effectiveness × implementation) cluster RCT has started in Dutch general practice (Hogervorst 2026, NCT06762275). It compares usual care with a diagnostic strategy that combines an externally validated 7-item prediction rule, CRP POCT and risk-specific management advice. Children aged 4-18 with acute abdominal pain (onset within 7 days) are stratified into low, intermediate and high risk. Low-risk children receive safety netting, high-risk children are referred immediately, and only intermediate-risk children are triaged with CRP POCT (CRP <10 mg/L → safety netting; 10-50 mg/L → reassessment or immediate referral, chosen by the GP's clinical judgment and the wishes of the child and parents; ≥50 mg/L → immediate referral). The primary outcome is new: it is neither sensitivity nor specificity but **"referral efficiency," the proportion of children without appendicitis who avoid referral over 30 days (target 88% → 95% among 566 children without appendicitis)**. The trial asks a primary-care question rather than competing on imaging accuracy: **can over-referral be reduced without increasing missed cases?** The implementation evaluation (reach / adoption / implementation / maintenance) runs in parallel, which anticipates the familiar problem of "accurate, but never adopted in practice." Results have not yet been reported (protocol stage). The strategy **puts low-risk children under safety netting, uses time as an ally, and triages them on reassessment**. The setting differs (primary care vs ER), but this is the same idea as Lastunen 2026's "observe and re-measure." Both answer the problem the same way: **use time to cut excess referrals and imaging**.

Differential diagnoses and pitfalls have also accumulated. In children, pinworm (Enterobius vermicularis) can mimic appendicitis (Jaffry 2026). A case of idiopathic encapsulating peritoneal sclerosis (abdominal cocoon) was misdiagnosed as appendicitis (Martzivanou 2026). **Stump appendicitis can occur even after a previous appendectomy** (Gupta 2026, 25-year review), so "appendix already removed" does not exclude appendicitis. The reverse pitfall also exists, where **appendicitis presents looking like another disease**. A case report describes a 2-day-old neonate (36 weeks' gestation, 2500 g) with abdominal distension, poor feeding and **bright red blood in the stool**. Necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) was suspected, and laparotomy found acute appendicitis (Kılıç 2026). Neonatal appendicitis is extremely rare, and the risk of delayed diagnosis and perforation is high. The authors urge clinicians to **include appendicitis in the differential for neonates with bloody stools, abdominal distension and signs of obstruction or ileus** (a single case report says nothing about frequency).

### Treatment
**Non-perforated (uncomplicated)**: both appendectomy and antibiotics-first are options. The standard approach is shared decision-making that takes into account whether an appendicolith is present (Talan & Di Saverio 2021). This framework has not changed, but the 2026 arrivals took the debate further in three directions. (1) RCT meta-analyses of antibiotics vs surgery now exist for **children and adolescents** as well (Allocati 2026), but a comment questioned how robust the conclusions are once prediction intervals are taken into account (Nasri & Dziri 2026). (2) One step further, **a meta-analysis of antibiotics vs observation without antibiotics asks "are antibiotics needed at all?"** (Lin 2026), and a comparison of optimal pediatric regimens followed (Kakar 2026). (3) On **cost-effectiveness**, conservative treatment comes out ahead: surgery costs 16.5-83% more (Farhad 2026). A group of narrative reviews is organizing patient selection and treatment algorithms (Rincon Mora / Baana / Lehovsky & Hall / Hong for children, all 2026). In children, **endoscopic retrograde appendicitis therapy (ERAT) has begun to be discussed as a third option after surgery and antibiotics** (Hong 2026). The evidence is growing so quickly that an evidence map tracking the whole field as a living systematic review has appeared (Kleindienst 2026). Surgery still holds the first-line position, with laparoscopy as the standard (Vidarsdottir 2025). Štefanová & Sákra 2026 review the history of treatment and current standards in line with the WSES 2025 guidelines and ACS guidance. They trace the progression from **conservative treatment, to open appendectomy through a McBurney incision, to laparoscopic appendectomy, the current gold standard**. They then locate the open questions in the **technical details** of laparoscopy: port selection, handling of the mesoappendix, stump closure method, and retrieval bags to prevent wound and intra-abdominal infection. Before surgery existed, treatment was conservative. The current antibiotics-first debate can therefore be read as **a conditional return to conservative management**, this time with antibiotics and imaging available.

A **fourth axis, time,** has also entered the debate. Most conventional RCTs report non-inferiority at 1 year. Kahana 2026 is **a meta-analysis restricted to studies with at least 2 years of follow-up**: 1635 studies were screened and 9 studies with 3883 patients were included (3 RCTs, 5 pediatric; median follow-up 33.6 months, maximum 312 months). It shows a different picture: **long-term NOM failure was 38.9% (95%CI 31.1-46.7), 44.4% (41.4-47.4) in RCTs only, and 36.3% (28.9-43.7) of patients underwent appendectomy after NOM**. The "80% success at 1 year" message **becomes "around 40% end up having surgery anyway" over several years**, which changes the numbers patients are given in shared decision-making. Štefanová & Sákra 2026 accept primary antibiotic therapy as "a reasonable alternative in selected patients," but they also state explicitly that **the long-term recurrence rate is considerable**. Guideline-aligned reviews are starting to take this figure as given. Costs, meanwhile, still favor NOM over the long term (**difference €1535, 95%CI -1892 to -1178**), which extends the evidence beyond the 12-month limit of Farhad 2026. Appendiceal neoplasms were found in about 0.3% of patients during NOM, which links to the neoplasm-risk axis discussed below. However, 6 non-RCTs are included and the definition of "failure" varies between studies. The 40% figure is therefore **a crude measure that says nothing about recurrence severity, QOL or satisfaction**.

**Complicated**: for perforation, phlegmon and abscess, **an individualized step-up strategy** is recommended. It runs from conservative treatment with IV antibiotics and percutaneous drainage, to appendectomy, and if needed to cecal base resection or ileocecal resection (Štefanová & Sákra 2026, WSES 2025-aligned). In children, laparoscopy is safe even when an abscess is present (Wang 2026, prospective cohort). After conservative treatment, **the main reason for interval appendectomy is shifting from "preventing recurrence" to "age-linked neoplasm risk plus selection based on imaging findings"** (Gosavi 2026; for children, the APSA systematic review, Sulkowski 2026). A letter to the editor has already responded to the APSA review (Bašković 2026), but it has no abstract, and we have not checked the full text to see what it disputes. The neoplasm-risk axis also extends beyond interval appendectomy. **In patients aged ≥40, colorectal neoplasia was found in 0.5-34.6% after appendicitis, with risk increased 1.2- to 38.5-fold** (Spota 2026, scoping review of 17 studies). All of these studies were retrospective, so the review makes no recommendation on standard follow-up and only proposes a framework for prospective studies. Perioperative management of "what comes after surgery" is also being optimized: in non-perforated gangrenous appendicitis, a single antibiotic dose may be as effective as continued postoperative therapy (Ozen 2026, antibiotic stewardship); in children with perforation, postoperative intra-abdominal abscess remains the main complication (Borca 2026); and an RCT of a modified ERAS protocol shortened length of stay (Calderón-Alvarado 2026). **Evidence-based nursing care**, covering pain management, infection prevention and discharge support (Stansell 2026), has been added to this list.

Before asking "how short" an antibiotic course can be, there is the question of "what to target." A systematic review of causative organisms in pediatric complicated appendicitis (Almeida-Gomes 2026, 12 years, 23 observational cohort studies) found **Escherichia coli to be the most common**, followed by Streptococcus, Bacteroides, Pseudomonas, Enterococcus and Klebsiella species. This applies the usual basis of empirical therapy (Enterobacteriaceae and anaerobes, with Pseudomonas and Enterococcus also in view) specifically to pediatric complicated disease. The stewardship question of whether a single dose is enough (Ozen 2026) and the discussion of postoperative abscess prevention (Borca 2026) both depend on this microbial picture. However, the authors themselves rate **the certainty of the evidence as low**. Specimen collection, microbiological methods and outcome reporting vary widely, which makes cross-study comparison and generalization difficult. Susceptibility patterns cannot be read from the abstract either. The finding is best treated as "a rough list of targets," not as something that directly changes regimen choice.

### Epidemiology and pathophysiology
Estimates of lifetime risk vary by source, from 6-7% (Vidarsdottir 2025) to 7-9% (Kleindienst 2026), with a peak at ages 10-19. Regional differences, diet and socioeconomic status have long been thought to play a role. A new review takes the dietary hypothesis down to mechanism and **formulates it as a "nutrition-microbiome-genetic axis"** (Ryoo 2026). This model of pathogenesis complements the luminal obstruction theory rather than replacing it. Consistent with this framework, **uncomplicated and complicated appendicitis have clearly different cytokine profiles**, with IL-6, HGF and MCP-1 raised in complicated disease (Han 2026, prospective, 113 patients). This gives immunological support to the view that complicated appendicitis is a different disease, not just an advanced stage of uncomplicated appendicitis, and that view in turn underpins NOM for uncomplicated disease. One study has also tested the microbiome axis at the level of which bacteria are present (Almeida-Gomes 2026, E. coli predominant in pediatric complicated disease). However, the quantitative pooling **included only studies that reported patient-level prevalence by culture**. Culture only shows which bacteria grow. It does not measure the same thing as the changes in gut microbial community structure that Ryoo 2026 describes, so the "microbiome" of the disease model and the "causative organisms" found in clinical specimens are not yet connected. In children, the link between nutritional status and perforation risk has also been studied. **Obesity or BMI alone does not predict perforation, whereas underweight and albumin/prealbumin measures are more consistently associated with complicated disease** (Borca 2026, 14 studies). The basic clinical point still holds: appendicitis can progress to perforation and abscess, so prompt diagnosis determines outcome. This matters most at the two ends of the age range. **In neonates, the condition is so rare that it is easily not considered, and the risk of delay and perforation is high** (Kılıç 2026). In primary care, most children with acute abdominal pain have benign, self-limiting conditions (Hogervorst 2026 assumes 88% referral efficiency under usual care). The gap between the prevalence reported in hospital populations and the prevalence seen in primary care is therefore itself becoming a design variable for diagnostic strategy. Findings also change with the time from onset to presentation, which is exactly why ∆AAS works (Lastunen 2026). This shows that appendicitis is **a process that changes over time**, not a single fixed picture. The same applies across populations: pregnancy changes how well inflammation-based scores perform (Tavakoli 2026), so **how findings should be read also depends on who the patient is**.

### Open questions
- Recurrence, QOL and satisfaction in the antibiotics-first group **beyond 5 years**. Kahana 2026 covered the period beyond 2 years (failure 38.9%, median 33.6 months), but the definition of "failure" varies between studies, and recurrence severity and patient experience remain unknown
- Large RCTs of antibiotics vs observation without antibiotics (raised by Lin 2026), which would re-examine what "antibiotics-first" should actually mean
- Robustness of the pediatric NOM recommendations: replication studies that address the prediction-interval problem (Nasri & Dziri 2026). These also need to be reconciled with the ~40% long-term failure rate (Kahana 2026; 5 of 9 studies pediatric)
- External validation of the continuous risk model pARC and its transfer to other populations. Does the finding that it outperforms fixed additive-point scores (Tchouala Tchakoute 2026) hold outside Europe and North America and in adults, and can it be used in practice once built into the EHR? (In adults, repeated AAS measurement is addressing the same gap by another route)
- **External validation of ∆AAS (observe and re-measure)**: the optimal observation time, whether the thresholds are valid (Lastunen 2026 is a secondary analysis of a single trial with n=83, with thresholds derived from its own data), whether it transfers to children and pregnant women, and whether the change over time can be built into pARC-type continuous models
- **Pregnancy-specific AIR cutoffs**: with 5 studies and I² 75.6%, the choice of cutoff changes specificity substantially (Tavakoli 2026). Thresholds by gestational age need to be set in prospective multicenter studies, and their place in triage before MRI needs to be defined
- The role of ERAT (endoscopic retrograde appendicitis therapy) in children: trials comparing it with surgery and antibiotics (raised by Hong 2026)
- Defining the age thresholds and imaging findings that indicate interval appendectomy (stratification by neoplasm risk)
- Colonoscopic follow-up after appendicitis in patients aged ≥40: prospective validation of who needs it and when (Spota 2026 proposes a framework)
- Predictors of failure other than an appendicolith: identifying complicated disease before surgery by combining biomarkers such as IL-6 (+HGF/MCP-1), imaging and AI
- Standardized research on causative organisms: multicenter studies with consistent specimen collection and culture/sequencing methods (called for by Almeida-Gomes 2026). Can susceptibility data be linked to support empirical regimens for pediatric complicated disease, and can changes in the gut microbiota (Ryoo 2026) be connected to the organisms found in clinical specimens?
- Multicenter, prospective external validation and implementation (EHR/PACS integration) of AI diagnostic models. Can studies report not only discrimination (AUC) but also **calibration, clinical utility by decision curve analysis, fairness and reproducibility** according to TRIPOD+AI / PROBAST+AI? (Bašković & Pogorelić 2026 provide a checklist and research agenda.) There is also no established standard yet for evaluating large language models used to support clinical decisions
- Does the primary-care prediction rule + CRP POCT actually reduce over-referral without increasing delayed diagnosis? Results of Hogervorst 2026 (NCT06762275) are awaited. Whether the findings apply to free-access health systems without gatekeeping (including Japan) has not been examined
