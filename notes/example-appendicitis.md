## 📌 Five-line summary
<!-- LN:SUMMARY:START -->
- Appendicitis is the most common cause of surgical acute abdomen. Lifetime risk is 6-9%, with a peak at 10-19 years. Its pathogenesis can be framed along a "nutrition-microbiome-genetic axis," and uncomplicated and complicated appendicitis may be "two different diseases" with distinct cytokine profiles (Ryoo / Han 2026). The microbial story is told in three layers: cultured organisms (E. coli is most frequent in pediatric complicated disease, low certainty; Almeida-Gomes 2026), the bacterial microbiota, and viruses. However, **both viral involvement and the mechanism of spontaneous resolution remain hypotheses** (Berikkhanov 2026)
- Diagnosis is converging on "**don't decide on a single test, don't decide at a single time point**." Alvarado cannot be used alone, the continuous pARC model outperforms fixed-point scores, and early equivocal cases should be **observed and re-measured** (single AAS AUROC 0.629 → ∆AAS 0.932). Moreover, **scores do not transfer across populations**: AIR, strong on specificity in children, reaches only AUC 0.752 in pregnant women (Tchouala Tchakoute / Lastunen / Tavakoli 2026)
- On imaging, in-hospital CT is the strongest test, MRI is the test of choice in pregnancy (sensitivity 0.95 / specificity 0.97), and POCUS is for rule-in only. The high AUCs of AI/ML **"reflect methodological fragility rather than clinical readiness"**: calibration, external validation and clinical utility go unreported (Bašković & Pogorelić 2026). The focus is shifting from an accuracy race to triage in primary care (Hogervorst 2026, NCT06762275)
- For non-perforated disease, surgery vs antibiotics is a matter of shared decision-making, but **the picture changes over time**. Beyond 2 years of follow-up, NOM failure is 38.9% and 36.3% go on to appendectomy, while the cost advantage (€1535) holds in the long term (Kahana 2026). Viral symptoms or a positive viral test are not reasons to withhold indicated antibiotics or surgery (Berikkhanov 2026). On the surgical side, laparoscopy is the gold standard (per WSES 2025, Štefanová 2026)
- Complicated disease calls for an **individualized step-up approach** (IV antibiotics + percutaneous drainage → resection). The decision axis for interval appendectomy is shifting to "age-linked neoplasm risk" (colorectal neoplasia after appendicitis in 0.5-34.6% of patients aged ≥40). The library has grown from 3 to 44 papers, a pace that calls for a "living review" of the same kind as the living evidence map (Kleindienst 2026)

## 🧵 Cross-cutting synthesis
<!-- LN:SYNTHESIS:START -->
### Diagnosis
The AHRQ systematic review of diagnostic accuracy for right lower quadrant pain (Dahabreh 2015, more than 1000 studies) still forms the backbone. Its conclusion: **"symptoms, physical signs and blood tests are insufficient on their own; confirmation comes from imaging."** CT is the strongest test across all populations, with sensitivity 0.96-1.0 and specificity 0.91-1.0. In pregnancy, MRI is the main alternative (Danawar 2026). An updated meta-analysis of 34 studies (Habiro Alves 2026) now gives this position **quantitative support: sensitivity 0.95 and specificity 0.97 (AUC 0.961)**. MRI is accordingly recommended as "the standard second-line test after an indeterminate ultrasound." The POCUS era has also put a number on the observation that "US is operator-dependent." POCUS performed by emergency physicians has sensitivity 0.78 and specificity 0.89, so **a positive scan can rule in, but a negative scan cannot exclude**, and accuracy varies with fellowship training (Kamal 2026, meta-analysis of 15 studies).

The role of clinical scores has likewise been clarified, from "adjunct" to **"never used alone: one component of a stepwise multimodal approach."** A review comparing Alvarado with alternative tools in children (Tchouala Tchakoute 2026, literature from 2016-2026) found that Alvarado has good sensitivity but **specificity of only 57-76% at a cutoff of ≥7**, so it cannot be used on its own. Among pediatric-specific scores, **PAS performs better on sensitivity and AIR on specificity, and AIR also discriminates well for complicated disease such as gangrene, phlegmon and perforation**. Furthermore, **continuous risk models (pARC) outperform fixed-point scores in pediatric cohorts**, moving scores from "adding up points" to "estimating probability." The review concludes that the best approach adds CRP, the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and selective ultrasound to this, and reserves CT for indeterminate cases.

However, **score performance does not transfer across populations**. AIR has been valued for its specificity in children. The first diagnostic-accuracy meta-analysis applying it to pregnant women (Tavakoli 2026, 5 studies, bivariate random-effects model) found only moderate performance: **pooled sensitivity 66.7% (95% CI 44.2-83.5), specificity 75.5% (40.0-93.5), AUC 0.752**, with a false-positive rate as high as 24.5%. Heterogeneity was large (I² 75.6%), and one of its sources was **the choice of cutoff**. The cutoff significantly shifts specificity but not sensitivity. Within a single study, raising the cutoff from ≥4 to ≥9 dropped sensitivity from 99.1% to 36.8% and raised specificity from 42.4% to 97.0%. Given the physiological changes in white cell count and CRP during pregnancy, it is not surprising that inflammation-based scores are unstable. The authors conclude that AIR is **an adjunct to clinical assessment rather than a stand-alone diagnostic**, and call for prospective multicenter studies to establish pregnancy-specific cutoffs. In pregnancy, the front-end arrangement of "narrow down with a score, decide with MRI" now has numerical support as well, although the evidence comes from only 5 studies and the confidence intervals are wide.

The September 2026 arrival added **the dimension of "time"** to this move away from stand-alone scores. Lastunen 2026 is a secondary analysis of the DIAMOND trial's observation arm, restricted to early, diagnostically difficult (early equivocal) adult cases (83 patients). **A single Adult Appendicitis Score (AAS) at presentation had an AUROC of 0.629 (95% CI 0.498-0.760), which is of little practical use.** By contrast, the **final AAS re-scored after brief observation rose to 0.936 (0.886-0.987), and the change in score during observation (∆AAS) to 0.932 (0.868-0.996)**. ∆CRP was intermediate at 0.796. With the thresholds given in the abstract (low ≤ -2 / intermediate -1 to 0 / high ≥ 1), **the negative predictive value in the low-probability group was 97% and the positive predictive value in the high-probability group was 94%**. In early equivocal abdominal pain, then, there are situations where **"measure again and look at the difference"** works better than adding tests. It is also a strategy that **reduces overdiagnosis and radiation exposure** from early CT. However, this is a secondary analysis of a single trial with n=83, the thresholds were derived from ROC analysis on the same data (no external validation), and the optimal observation time is not given. Scores have now moved in two steps: from "adding up points" to "estimating probability" to "**a function of time**." This shift reflects the same design principle as the primary-care strategy described below: "don't decide on a single test."

New biomarkers are also accumulating. IL-6, considered promising in children (Tan & Bo 2026), is supported by prospective adult data. In a 48-cytokine analysis, complicated appendicitis had significantly higher IL-6, HGF and MCP-1, and IL-6 alone predicted complicated disease with AUC 0.785 (Han 2026). AI models follow (AUC 0.85-0.96; CT deep learning achieves the highest accuracy, but studies are mostly retrospective and single-center and lack external validation; Ismayilzada 2026). A critical methodological review has also appeared that questions these "high AUCs" themselves (Bašković & Pogorelić 2026). It surveys clinical scores, machine learning, deep learning and large language models for pediatric appendicitis across **the entire care pathway: not only binary diagnosis but also severity stratification, prediction of NOM response, imaging stewardship and postoperative course**. Appraised against TRIPOD+AI / PROBAST+AI criteria, **reported performance is skewed toward discrimination, while calibration, clinical utility, external validation, fairness and reproducibility are inconsistently reported or missing**. The authors state that "the recurring pattern of very high accuracy reflects **methodological fragility** more than clinical readiness," and include a checklist for critically reading prediction studies. As scores move toward "estimating probability," the key question becomes not how well they rank patients (AUC) but **whether the probabilities they output are accurate (calibration)**. That is exactly where the gap lies, and external validation of pARC and ∆AAS needs the same yardstick. The same caution applies to a new candidate variable, the "viral prodrome." Whether cold-like preceding symptoms or a positive viral test add independent information to the diagnosis or prognosis of appendicitis is **not established**, and the authors treat it only as a candidate research variable (Berikkhanov 2026, see below).

Yet this whole accuracy discussion has **assumed "patients who have already reached the hospital or ER."** The August 2026 arrival moves the focus one step earlier, to **the point of entry in primary care**. A hybrid type 1 (effectiveness × implementation) cluster RCT has begun in Dutch general practice (Hogervorst 2026, NCT06762275). It compares usual care with a diagnostic strategy that bundles an externally validated 7-item prediction rule, CRP POCT and risk-specific management advice. Children aged 4-18 with acute abdominal pain (onset within 7 days) are stratified into low, intermediate and high risk. Low-risk children receive safety netting, high-risk children are referred immediately, and only intermediate-risk children are triaged by CRP POCT:

| CRP POCT | Management |
|---|---|
| <10 mg/L | Safety netting |
| 10-50 mg/L | Reassessment or immediate referral, chosen by the GP's clinical judgment and the child's/parents' preferences |
| ≥50 mg/L | Immediate referral |

What is new is the primary outcome. It is neither sensitivity nor specificity but **"referral efficiency": the proportion of children without appendicitis who were not referred within 30 days (target 88%→95% among 566 non-appendicitis children)**. The trial is not a race for imaging accuracy. It asks the primary-care question of **whether over-referral can be reduced without raising the threshold for missed cases**. It also runs an implementation evaluation (reach / adoption / implementation / maintenance) in parallel, anticipating the problem of "accuracy achieved but not adopted in practice." Results are not yet reported (protocol stage). The structure **places low-risk children under safety netting, uses the passage of time, and triages on reassessment**. This mirrors Lastunen 2026's "observe and re-measure," even though the settings differ (primary care vs ER). Both studies answer the same way: **using time to cut excess referrals and imaging**.

Differential diagnoses and pitfalls are also accumulating. Pinworm (Enterobius vermicularis) can mimic appendicitis in children (Jaffry 2026), and idiopathic encapsulating peritoneal sclerosis (abdominal cocoon) has been misdiagnosed as appendicitis (Martzivanou 2026). **Stump appendicitis can occur even after appendectomy** (Gupta 2026, 25-year review), so a prior appendectomy does not exclude appendicitis. In the context of viral illness, other conditions that look just like appendicitis (**clinical phenocopies**) must be distinguished and not misread as "the virus caused appendicitis" (Berikkhanov 2026). There are also pitfalls in the opposite direction, where **appendicitis presents in the guise of another disease**. Kılıç 2026 reports a 2-day-old neonate (36 weeks' gestation, 2500 g) with abdominal distension, poor feeding and **fresh rectal bleeding**. Necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) was suspected, and laparotomy revealed acute appendicitis. Neonatal appendicitis is extremely rare and carries high risks of delayed diagnosis and perforation. The authors urge that it be **included in the differential for neonates with rectal bleeding accompanied by abdominal distension or signs of bowel obstruction/ileus**. As a single case report, it cannot speak to frequency.

### Treatment
**Non-perforated (uncomplicated)**: both appendectomy and antibiotics-first are options. The established approach is shared decision-making informed by whether an appendicolith is present (Talan & Di Saverio 2021). This framework is unchanged, but the 2026 arrivals took the debate further in three directions.

1. **Children and adolescents**: a meta-analysis of RCTs comparing antibiotics with surgery has appeared for this group as well (Allocati 2026). A commentary argued that its conclusions are less robust once prediction intervals are considered (Nasri & Dziri 2026).
2. **Are antibiotics needed at all?** A **meta-analysis of antibiotics vs no-antibiotic observation** (Lin 2026) has appeared, followed by a comparison of optimal pediatric regimens (Kakar 2026). Why some cases settle even without antibiotics is not yet explained biologically. The idea that they resolve on their own because they are viral is unproven (Berikkhanov 2026, see below).
3. **Cost-effectiveness**: conservative treatment is favored, with surgery 16.5-83% more costly (Farhad 2026).

Patient selection and treatment algorithms are being organized in a group of narrative reviews (Rincon Mora, Baana, Lehovsky & Hall, and Hong for children, all 2026). In children, **endoscopic retrograde appendicitis therapy (ERAT) has begun to be discussed as a third option after surgery and antibiotics** (Hong 2026). The evidence is growing so fast that an evidence map now tracks the whole field as a living systematic review (Kleindienst 2026). Surgery remains first-line, with laparoscopy as standard (Vidarsdottir 2025). Štefanová & Sákra 2026 review the history of treatment and current standards in line with the WSES 2025 guidelines and ACS guidance. They trace treatment from **conservative management, to open appendectomy via a McBurney incision, to today's gold standard, laparoscopic appendectomy**. They then place the open questions in the **technical details** of laparoscopy: port selection, handling of the mesoappendix, stump closure method, and use of retrieval bags to prevent wound and intra-abdominal infection. Treatment began with conservative management before the surgical era. Today's antibiotics-first debate can therefore be read as **a conditional return to "conservative" care**, now with antibiotics and imaging. That return has limits: **viral symptoms or a positive viral test are no grounds for withholding indicated antibiotics or surgery** (Berikkhanov 2026).

A **fourth axis, time**, has now been added to this debate. Most RCTs so far have reported non-inferiority at 1 year. Kahana 2026 is **a meta-analysis restricted to studies with ≥2 years of follow-up**: 1635 studies screened, 9 studies and 3883 patients included (3 RCTs, 5 pediatric), median follow-up 33.6 months, maximum 312 months. It gives a different picture:

| Long-term outcome of NOM | % (95% CI) |
|---|---|
| Failure, all studies | 38.9 (31.1-46.7) |
| Failure, RCTs only | 44.4 (41.4-47.4) |
| Went on to appendectomy after NOM | 36.3 (28.9-43.7) |

In other words, the "80% success at 1 year" message becomes, **over several years, "around 40% end up having surgery anyway."** This changes the numbers given to patients in SDM. Štefanová & Sákra 2026 likewise accept primary antibiotic therapy as "a reasonable alternative in selected patients" but state explicitly that **long-term recurrence rates are considerable**. Guideline-based reviews are thus starting to take this figure as given. Meanwhile, the cost advantage of NOM persists in the long term (**difference €1535, 95% CI -1892 to -1178**), extending Farhad 2026, which was limited to 12 months. Appendiceal neoplasms were found in about 0.3% of patients during NOM, which connects to the neoplasm-risk axis below. However, 6 of the 9 studies were non-randomized, and the definition of "failure" varies between studies. This 40% figure is therefore **a crude indicator that does not capture severity of recurrence, QOL or satisfaction**.

**Complicated**: for perforation, phlegmon and abscess, an **individualized step-up strategy** is recommended (Štefanová & Sákra 2026, per WSES 2025). It runs from conservative management with IV antibiotics and percutaneous drainage, to appendectomy, and if needed to cecal base resection or ileocecal resection. In children, laparoscopy is safe even when an abscess is present (Wang 2026, prospective cohort). After conservative management, **the decision on interval appendectomy is shifting from "preventing recurrence" to "selection based on age-linked neoplasm risk plus imaging findings"** (Gosavi 2026; for children, the APSA systematic review, Sulkowski 2026). A letter to the editor on the APSA review has already appeared (Bašković 2026), but it has no abstract, and the points it disputes have not been verified from the full text. The neoplasm-risk axis also extends beyond interval appendectomy. **In patients aged ≥40, colorectal neoplasia is reportedly detected in 0.5-34.6% after appendicitis, with a 1.2- to 38.5-fold increase in risk** (Spota 2026, scoping review of 17 studies). All included studies are retrospective, so the review stops short of recommending standard follow-up and instead proposes a framework for prospective studies.

Perioperative management is also being refined after surgery:

- Single-dose antibiotics may be equivalent to continued postoperative antibiotics in non-perforated gangrenous appendicitis (Ozen 2026, antibiotic stewardship).
- Postoperative intra-abdominal abscess remains the main complication of perforated appendicitis in children (Borca 2026).
- An RCT of a modified ERAS protocol shortened hospital stay (Calderón-Alvarado 2026).
- **Evidence-based nursing care** covering pain management, infection prevention and discharge support has also been integrated (Stansell 2026).

Before asking "how short" an antibiotic course can be, there is the question of "what to target." A systematic review of causative organisms in pediatric complicated appendicitis (Almeida-Gomes 2026, 12 years, 23 observational cohort studies) found that **Escherichia coli was the most frequent**, followed by Streptococcus, Bacteroides, Pseudomonas, Enterococcus and Klebsiella species. This restates the basis of empirical therapy specifically for pediatric complicated disease: Enterobacteriaceae and anaerobes as the core, with Pseudomonas and Enterococcus also in view. The stewardship question of whether a single dose suffices (Ozen 2026) and the discussion of preventing postoperative abscess (Borca 2026) also rest on this microbial profile. However, the authors themselves rate **the certainty of evidence as low**. Specimen collection, microbiological methods and outcome reporting vary widely, which makes cross-study comparison and generalization difficult. Susceptibility patterns cannot be read from the abstract either. The review is best read as a rough list of target organisms rather than a finding that directly changes regimen choice.

### Epidemiology and pathophysiology
Lifetime risk varies between sources from 6-7% (Vidarsdottir 2025) to 7-9% (Kleindienst 2026), peaking at 10-19 years. Regional differences, diet and socioeconomic status have long been suggested to play a role. A review has now taken this dietary hypothesis down to the level of mechanism, **formulating it as a "nutrition-microbiome-genetic axis"** (Ryoo 2026). This pathophysiological model complements rather than replaces the luminal-obstruction theory. In line with this model, **uncomplicated and complicated appendicitis have clearly different cytokine profiles**, with IL-6, HGF and MCP-1 elevated in complicated disease (Han 2026, prospective, 113 patients). This gives immunological support to the view that complicated disease is a different disease, not simply a more advanced form of uncomplicated disease. That view in turn provides the theoretical basis for why NOM works in uncomplicated disease. A study has also examined the microbiome axis at the level of "which bacteria are present" (Almeida-Gomes 2026, with E. coli predominant in pediatric complicated disease). However, its quantitative pooling was **limited to studies reporting patient-level prevalence by culture methods**. Culture shows which organisms grow. It does not measure the shifts in gut microbial community structure that Ryoo 2026 describes. The "microbiome" of the pathophysiological model and the "causative organisms" found in clinical specimens are not yet linked.

A framework has also been proposed for interpreting findings about **viruses**, which may act before or alongside bacteria. Berikkhanov 2026, a structured narrative synthesis of 82 sources, points out that viral findings in appendicitis have been interpreted without distinguishing four scenarios:

1. **Direct infection** of appendiceal tissue
2. **Host responses** to systemic infection
3. Other conditions that look like appendicitis (**clinical phenocopies**)
4. Effects of the intestinal **virome**

When these are separated, direct viral injury has been documented **only in rare clinicopathological settings**. Tissue studies **tend to argue against** the view that direct adenovirus infection is frequent. It is plausible that systemic infection triggers lymphoid activation, edema and early microvascular injury that lead to appendicitis. This **has not been demonstrated as a causal chain in humans**, however. Clinical studies do document that some uncomplicated cases settle without antibiotics (cf. Lin 2026), but **there is no evidence that they resolved because they were viral**. The authors classify viral prodromes as candidate research variables whose independent diagnostic and prognostic value has not been established. They call for prospective studies linking the timeline of infection to local inflammation and outcomes. The paper presents no new data, and its search and quality-appraisal methods cannot be determined from the abstract. Its value lies in **a framework that separates observed findings from competing causal hypotheses**.

Taken together with the causative-organism review, the "microbes" in appendicitis pathophysiology are discussed in three layers: **culturable bacteria, the community structure of the microbiota, and viruses (including the virome)**. None of these layers has yet been linked over time to local inflammation. "Appendicitis that resolves on its own" is emerging as a property of the uncomplicated form, as the two-diseases view (Han 2026) suggests, but its biology remains unknown.

In children, the relationship between nutritional status and perforation risk has also been examined. **Obesity or BMI alone does not predict perforation, whereas underweight and low albumin/prealbumin are more consistently associated with complicated disease** (Borca 2026, 14 studies). The core clinical lesson is unchanged: because appendicitis can progress to perforation and abscess, prompt diagnosis determines prognosis. This matters most at the two extremes of age. **In neonates, the condition is so rare that it is less likely to come to mind, and the risks of delay and perforation are high** (Kılıç 2026). In primary care, most children with acute abdominal pain have benign, self-limiting conditions (Hogervorst 2026 assumes a referral efficiency of 88% under usual care). The prevalence seen in hospital populations therefore differs from the prevalence seen in primary care, and that gap is itself a design variable for diagnostic strategy. Findings also change depending on how long after onset a patient presents, which is exactly why ∆AAS works (Lastunen 2026). This shows that appendicitis is not a single fixed clinical picture but **a process that changes over time**. The same logic applies to populations. Pregnancy changes the performance of inflammation-based scores (Tavakoli 2026), which shows that **how findings should be read also depends on who the patient is**.

### Open questions
- **Recurrence, QOL and satisfaction beyond 5 years** in the antibiotics-first group. Kahana 2026 has filled in the picture beyond 2 years (failure 38.9%, median follow-up 33.6 months), but the definition of "failure" varies across studies. The severity of recurrence and the patient experience remain unknown
- A large RCT of antibiotics vs no-antibiotic observation (raised by Lin 2026), to re-examine what "antibiotics-first" should actually involve
- **The biology of spontaneous resolution and the role of viruses**. What is different about uncomplicated disease that settles without antibiotics? Do viral prodromes or positive viral tests carry independent diagnostic or prognostic value? Prospective studies linking the timeline of infection to local inflammation and outcomes are needed (framework proposed by Berikkhanov 2026)
- Robustness of the recommendation for NOM in children. Replication is needed that addresses the prediction-interval problem (Nasri & Dziri 2026). These findings also need to be reconciled with the ~40% long-term failure rate (Kahana 2026, in which 5 of 9 studies were pediatric)
- External validation of the continuous risk model pARC and its transfer to other populations. Does the finding that it outperforms fixed-point scores (Tchouala Tchakoute 2026) hold outside Europe and North America and in adults? Can it be used in practice once built into the EHR? (In adults, repeated AAS measurement is addressing the same gap by a different route)
- **External validation of ∆AAS (observe and re-measure)**. Open issues are the optimal observation time and the validity of the thresholds: Lastunen 2026 is a secondary analysis of a single trial with n=83, and its thresholds were derived from the same data. Transfer to children and pregnant women also needs testing, as does whether the change over time can be built into pARC-type continuous models
- **Pregnancy-specific AIR cutoffs**. Across 5 studies with I² 75.6%, the cutoff strongly shifts specificity (Tavakoli 2026). Prospective multicenter studies are needed to set gestational-age-specific thresholds and to decide how AIR fits into triage before MRI
- The role of ERAT (endoscopic retrograde appendicitis therapy) in children. Comparative trials against surgery and antibiotics are needed (raised by Hong 2026)
- Quantifying the age thresholds and imaging findings that should determine indications for interval appendectomy (stratification of neoplasm risk)
- Colonoscopic follow-up after appendicitis in patients aged ≥40. Indications and timing need prospective validation (framework proposed by Spota 2026)
- Predictors of NOM failure other than appendicolith. Can complicated disease be identified before surgery by combining biomarkers such as IL-6 (+HGF/MCP-1) with imaging and AI?
- Standardization of research on causative organisms. Multicenter studies with harmonized specimen collection and culture/sequencing methods are needed (called for by Almeida-Gomes 2026). Can linking organisms to susceptibility data provide an evidence base for empirical regimens in pediatric complicated disease? Can the organisms found in clinical specimens be connected to shifts in the gut microbiota (Ryoo 2026) and the virome (Berikkhanov 2026)?
- Multicenter, prospective external validation and implementation (EHR/PACS integration) of AI diagnostic models. Can studies report not only discrimination (AUC) but also **calibration, clinical utility via decision curves, fairness and reproducibility**, in line with TRIPOD+AI / PROBAST+AI? (Bašković & Pogorelić 2026 provide a checklist and research agenda.) Standards for evaluating large language models used for clinical decision support are also not yet established
- Does the primary-care prediction rule plus CRP POCT actually reduce over-referral without increasing delayed diagnosis? Results of Hogervorst 2026 (NCT06762275) are awaited. Whether the findings apply to free-access health systems without gatekeeping (including Japan) has not been examined
