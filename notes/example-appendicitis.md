## 📌 Five-line summary
<!-- LN:SUMMARY:START -->
- Appendicitis is the most common surgical acute abdomen. Lifetime risk is 6-9%, peaking at age 10-19. Its pathogenesis can be framed as a "nutrition-microbiome-genetic axis." Uncomplicated and complicated appendicitis have different cytokine profiles and may be "two different diseases" (Ryoo / Han 2026). Microbes are discussed in three layers: cultured bacteria (E. coli most common in pediatric complicated cases, low certainty, Almeida-Gomes 2026), the microbiota, and viruses. However, **both viral involvement and the mechanism of spontaneous resolution remain hypotheses** (Berikkhanov 2026)
- Diagnosis is converging on "**don't decide on a single test, don't decide at a single time point**." Alvarado cannot be used alone, the continuous pARC outperforms fixed-point scoring, and early equivocal cases should be **observed and re-scored** (single-time AAS AUROC 0.629 → ∆AAS 0.932). Moreover, **scores do not travel across populations**: AIR, strong on specificity in children, reaches only AUC 0.752 in pregnant women (Tchouala Tchakoute / Lastunen / Tavakoli 2026)
- For imaging, in-hospital CT is strongest, MRI is the choice in pregnancy (sensitivity 0.95 / specificity 0.97), and POCUS is for rule-in only. The high AUCs of AI/ML reflect **"methodological fragility rather than clinical readiness"**: calibration, external validation, and clinical utility go unreported (Bašković & Pogorelić 2026). The arena is shifting from accuracy contests to triage in primary care (Hogervorst 2026, NCT06762275)
- Surgery vs antibiotics for non-perforated disease is a matter of shared decision-making, but **the picture changes over time**. Beyond 2 years of follow-up, NOM failure is 38.9% and 36.3% eventually undergo appendectomy, yet NOM keeps a €1535 cost advantage long-term (Kahana 2026). Viral symptoms or positive viral tests are no reason to withhold indicated antibiotics or surgery (Berikkhanov 2026). On the surgical side, laparoscopy is the gold standard (per WSES 2025, Štefanová 2026)
- Complicated disease calls for an **individualized step-up** approach (IV antibiotics + percutaneous drainage → resection). For postoperative antibiotics, **3 days oral is non-inferior to IV** (abscess 6.8% vs 4.9%, stay −41 h, readmission +4.7%, Abdirahman Mohamud 2026). The decision axis for interval appendectomy is shifting toward age-linked neoplasm risk: appendiceal neoplasm is found in 12.2% of IA cases, **15.2% at ≥40 vs 2.1% below** (Wu 2026; threshold not established). The library has grown from 3 to 46 papers, a pace that calls for a "living review" like the living evidence map (Kleindienst 2026)

## 🧵 Cross-cutting synthesis
<!-- LN:SYNTHESIS:START -->
### Diagnosis
An AHRQ systematic review covered the diagnostic accuracy of all approaches to right lower quadrant pain (Dahabreh 2015, over 1000 studies). Its conclusion is still the backbone: **"symptoms, physical findings, and blood tests are insufficient on their own; confirmation comes from imaging."** CT is the strongest test in every population, with sensitivity 0.96-1.0 and specificity 0.91-1.0. In pregnancy, MRI is the main alternative (Danawar 2026). An updated meta-analysis of 34 studies (Habiro Alves 2026) now gives this position **quantitative support: sensitivity 0.95 and specificity 0.97 (AUC 0.961)**. It recommends MRI as "the standard second-line modality after an inconclusive ultrasound." The point that "US is operator-dependent" has been quantified in the POCUS era. POCUS by emergency physicians has sensitivity 0.78 and specificity 0.89, so **a positive result can rule in but a negative one cannot rule out**, and accuracy varies with fellowship training (Kamal 2026, meta-analysis of 15 studies).

The role of clinical scores has also been clarified, from "adjunct" to **"never used alone; one component of a stepwise multimodal approach."** A review comparing Alvarado with alternative tools in children (Tchouala Tchakoute 2026, literature from 2016-2026) found that Alvarado has good sensitivity but **only 57-76% specificity at a cutoff ≥7**, so it cannot be used alone. Among pediatric-specific scores, **PAS wins on sensitivity and AIR on specificity, and AIR is also good at identifying complicated disease such as gangrene, phlegmon, and perforation**. Furthermore, **a continuous risk model (pARC) outperforms fixed-point scores in pediatric cohorts**. This moves scores from "adding up points" to "estimating probability." The review's conclusion is that the best approach adds CRP, the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and selective ultrasound on top of this, and reserves CT for indeterminate cases.

However, **score performance does not travel across populations**. AIR was praised for its specificity in children. The first diagnostic accuracy meta-analysis of AIR in pregnant women (Tavakoli 2026, 5 studies, bivariate random-effects model) found only moderate performance: **pooled sensitivity 66.7% (95%CI 44.2-83.5), specificity 75.5% (40.0-93.5), AUC 0.752**, with a false-positive rate as high as 24.5%. Heterogeneity is large (I² 75.6%), and one source of it is **the choice of cutoff**. The cutoff significantly shifts specificity but not sensitivity. Even within a single study, raising the cutoff from ≥4 to ≥9 drops sensitivity from 99.1% to 36.8% and raises specificity from 42.4% to 97.0%. White cell count and CRP change physiologically in pregnancy, so it is natural for inflammation-based scores to waver. The authors conclude that AIR is **an adjunct to clinical assessment, not a standalone diagnostic**, and call for prospective multicenter studies to set pregnancy-specific cutoffs. In pregnancy, the front-end approach of "narrow with a score, decide with MRI" is now backed by numbers as well. However, the analysis rests on only 5 studies, and the confidence intervals are wide.

The 2026-09 arrival added **a dimension of "time"** to this move away from scores used alone. Lastunen 2026 is a secondary analysis of the DIAMOND trial's observation arm, restricted to 83 adults with early equivocal signs. **A single Adult Appendicitis Score (AAS) at presentation had an AUROC of 0.629 (95%CI 0.498-0.760), which is close to useless.** In contrast, **the final AAS, re-scored after brief observation, rose sharply to 0.936 (0.886-0.987), and the change in score during observation (∆AAS) to 0.932 (0.868-0.996)** (∆CRP was intermediate at 0.796). With the thresholds given in the abstract (low ≤ −2 / intermediate −1 to 0 / high ≥ 1), **the low-probability group has an NPV of 97% and the high-probability group a PPV of 94%**. So for early equivocal abdominal pain, **"measuring again and looking at the difference"** sometimes works better than adding tests. It is also a strategy for **reducing overdiagnosis and radiation exposure** from early CT. However, this is a secondary analysis of a single trial with n=83. The thresholds were drawn from ROC analysis of the same data (no external validation), and the optimal observation time is not reported. Scores have moved in two steps: from "adding up points" to "estimating probability" to "**a function of time**." This reflects the same design philosophy as the primary care strategy described below: "don't decide on a single test."

New biomarkers are also emerging. IL-6, promising in children (Tan & Bo 2026), is now supported by prospective adult data. In a 48-cytokine analysis, IL-6, HGF, and MCP-1 were significantly higher in complicated appendicitis, and IL-6 alone predicted complicated disease with an AUC of 0.785 (Han 2026). AI models follow (AUC 0.85-0.96, Ismayilzada 2026). CT deep learning is the most accurate, but most of these models are retrospective and single-center and lack external validation. A critical methodological review has now questioned these "high AUCs" themselves (Bašković & Pogorelić 2026). It surveys clinical scores, machine learning, deep learning, and large language models for pediatric appendicitis across **the whole care pathway: not only binary diagnosis but also severity stratification, prediction of NOM response, imaging stewardship, and the postoperative course**. Appraised against TRIPOD+AI / PROBAST+AI, **reported performance is skewed toward discrimination, while calibration, clinical utility, external validation, fairness, and reproducibility are reported inconsistently or not at all**. The authors write that "the recurring pattern of very high accuracy reflects **methodological fragility** more than clinical readiness," and add a checklist for critically reading prediction studies. As scores move toward "estimating probability," the key question shifts from how well they rank patients (AUC) to **whether the probabilities they give are correct (calibration)**, and that is exactly where reporting is missing. External validation of pARC and ∆AAS needs the same yardstick. The same caution applies to a new candidate variable, the "viral prodrome." Whether preceding cold-like symptoms or positive viral tests add independent information to diagnosis or prognosis is **not established**, and the authors treat it only as a candidate research variable (Berikkhanov 2026, see below).

Yet this accuracy debate has always **assumed "patients who have already reached the hospital or ER."** The 2026-08 arrival moves the arena one step earlier, to **the primary care entry point**. A hybrid type 1 (effectiveness × implementation) cluster RCT has begun in Dutch general practice (Hogervorst 2026, NCT06762275). It compares usual care with a diagnostic strategy that combines an externally validated 7-item prediction rule, CRP POCT, and risk-specific management advice. Children aged 4-18 with acute abdominal pain (onset within 7 days) are stratified into low, intermediate, and high risk:
- Low risk: safety netting
- High risk: immediate referral
- Intermediate risk: triage by CRP POCT
  - CRP <10 mg/L: safety netting
  - CRP 10-50 mg/L: re-evaluation or immediate referral, chosen by GP clinical judgment and child/parent preference
  - CRP ≥50 mg/L: immediate referral

What is new is the primary outcome, which is neither sensitivity nor specificity but **"referral efficiency": the proportion of children without appendicitis who were not referred over 30 days (target 88%→95% in 566 non-appendicitis children)**. The question is not about imaging accuracy. It is a primary-care question: **can over-referral be reduced without raising the miss threshold?** The parallel implementation evaluation (reach / adoption / implementation / maintenance) also anticipates the problem of "accurate, but never adopted in practice." Results have not yet been reported (protocol stage). The trial **puts low-risk children on safety netting, lets time work in its favor, and triages at re-evaluation**. The setting differs (primary care vs ER), but this connects directly to Lastunen 2026's "observe and re-score." Both answer the same way: **use time to cut excess referrals and imaging**.

Differentials and pitfalls have accumulated too. Pinworm (Enterobius vermicularis) can mimic appendicitis in children (Jaffry 2026), and a case of idiopathic encapsulating peritoneal sclerosis (abdominal cocoon) was misdiagnosed as appendicitis (Martzivanou 2026). **Stump appendicitis can occur even after appendectomy** (Gupta 2026, 25-year review), so "appendix already removed = appendicitis excluded" does not hold. In viral illness, a condition that looks just like appendicitis (**clinical phenocopy**) must be distinguished, rather than misread as "the virus caused appendicitis" (Berikkhanov 2026). There is also the reverse pitfall: **appendicitis that presents as another disease**. Kılıç 2026 reports a 2-day-old neonate (36 weeks' gestation, 2500 g) with abdominal distension, poor feeding, and **bright red rectal bleeding**. Necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) was suspected, but laparotomy revealed acute appendicitis. Neonatal appendicitis is extremely rare, with a high risk of delayed diagnosis and perforation. The authors urge that it be **included in the differential for neonates with rectal bleeding plus abdominal distension and signs of obstruction/ileus** (a single case report, so frequency cannot be inferred).

### Treatment
**Non-perforated (uncomplicated)**: Both appendectomy and antibiotics-first are options, and the standard approach is shared decision-making that takes into account whether an appendicolith is present (Talan & Di Saverio 2021). This backbone is unchanged, but 2026 arrivals pushed the debate in three directions.
1. RCT meta-analyses of antibiotics vs surgery have now appeared for **children and adolescents** (Allocati 2026). A comment argued that once prediction intervals are taken into account, the robustness of the conclusion is questionable (Nasri & Dziri 2026).
2. Going a step further, **a meta-analysis of antibiotics vs observation without antibiotics asks "are antibiotics needed at all?"** (Lin 2026). A comparison of pediatric antibiotic regimens followed (Kakar 2026). Why some cases resolve without antibiotics has not been explained, and the idea that they subside naturally because they are viral is unproven (Berikkhanov 2026, see below).
3. **Cost-effectiveness** favors conservative treatment (surgery costs 16.5-83% more, Farhad 2026).

A group of 2026 narrative reviews is organizing patient selection and algorithms (Rincon Mora / Baana / Lehovsky & Hall / Hong for children). In children, **endoscopic retrograde appendicitis therapy (ERAT) is starting to be discussed as a third option after surgery and antibiotics** (Hong 2026). Evidence is growing so fast that an evidence map now tracks the whole field as a living systematic review (Kleindienst 2026). Surgery remains first-line, and laparoscopy is the standard (Vidarsdottir 2025). Štefanová & Sákra 2026 review the history of treatment and current standards, following the WSES 2025 guidelines and ACS guidance. Treatment moved from **conservative management → open appendectomy through a McBurney incision → laparoscopic appendectomy, the current gold standard**. The open debates are now about laparoscopic **technical details**: port selection, handling of the mesoappendix, stump closure method, and retrieval bags to prevent wound and intra-abdominal infection. Before surgery existed, treatment started from conservative management. Today's antibiotics-first debate can be read as **a conditional return to conservative management, now backed by antibiotics and imaging**. This return has its limits: **viral symptoms or positive viral tests are no grounds for withholding indicated antibiotics or surgery** (Berikkhanov 2026).

A **fourth axis, time,** has also been added to the debate. Most earlier RCTs reported non-inferiority at 1 year. Kahana 2026 meta-analyzed **only studies with ≥2 years of follow-up**: 9 of 1635 screened studies, 3883 patients, including 3 RCTs and 5 pediatric studies, with median follow-up 33.6 months (maximum 312 months). The picture is different:
- Long-term NOM failure: **38.9% (95%CI 31.1-46.7)**
- Long-term NOM failure, RCTs only: **44.4% (41.4-47.4)**
- Appendectomy after NOM: **36.3% (28.9-43.7)**

The "80% success at 1 year" message thus **becomes, over several years, "around 40% end up having surgery anyway."** The numbers given to patients in SDM change. The same review (Štefanová & Sákra 2026) accepts primary antibiotic therapy as "a reasonable alternative in selected patients," but also states that **long-term recurrence rates are considerably high**. Guideline-based reviews are now starting to take this figure as given. Meanwhile, NOM keeps its cost advantage over the long term (**difference €1535, 95%CI −1892 to −1178**). This goes beyond the limitation of Farhad 2026, whose data stopped at 12 months. About 0.3% of patients on NOM are found to have appendiceal neoplasms, which connects to the neoplasm-risk axis discussed below. However, 6 non-RCTs are included and the definition of "failure" varies between studies. The 40% figure is therefore **a crude indicator that says nothing about recurrence severity, QOL, or satisfaction**.

**Complicated**: For perforation, phlegmon, and abscess, an **individualized step-up strategy** is recommended (Štefanová & Sákra 2026, per WSES 2025). It runs from conservative treatment with IV antibiotics and percutaneous drainage, to appendectomy, and if needed to cecal base or ileocecal resection. In children, laparoscopy is safe even when an abscess is present (Wang 2026, prospective cohort). After conservative treatment, **the decision on interval appendectomy is shifting from "preventing recurrence" to "selection based on age-linked neoplasm risk + imaging findings"** (Gosavi 2026; for children, the APSA systematic review, Sulkowski 2026). A letter to the editor on the APSA review has already been published (Bašković 2026). It has no abstract, and we have not checked the full text to see which points it disputes.

Wu 2026 now gives this "neoplasm risk" **age-specific numbers** in adults for the first time. It is a systematic review and meta-analysis of adults who had interval appendectomy (IA) after complicated appendicitis: 12 studies, 1340 patients, 162 neoplasms, random-effects model, GRADE assessment. Pathologically confirmed appendiceal neoplasms were found in **12.2% (pooled), nearly 1 in 8**. Mucinous neoplasms were the most common histology. In the 3 studies/subsets with age-specific data, the difference was stark:

| Age | Appendiceal neoplasm |
|---|---|
| Under 40 | 2.1% |
| 40 and above | 15.2% |

(Q = 32.53, p < 0.0001.) This gives numbers to the shift in decision-making that Gosavi 2026 describes. However, the denominator is "patients selected for IA whose pathology was examined," **not all conservatively treated patients**, as the authors themselves state. If patients suspected of having a neoplasm are more likely to be sent for IA, the rate is biased upward. The age analysis rests on only 3 studies with inconsistent age cut-points. The authors therefore **avoid a blanket age-based recommendation and offer the data as input for individual patient selection**. The neoplasm rate during NOM for uncomplicated disease is about 0.3% (Kahana 2026), an order of magnitude lower. That gap reflects differences in disease type (uncomplicated vs complicated) and in population (all NOM patients vs patients selected for IA).

This neoplasm-risk axis also extends beyond the appendix, to the colon. Spota 2026 (scoping review of 17 studies) reports that **in patients aged 40 or older, colorectal neoplasms are found in 0.5-34.6% after appendicitis, with risk raised 1.2-38.5-fold**. All the studies are retrospective, so the review stops short of recommending standard follow-up and instead proposes a framework for prospective research. Both reviews happen to draw the line at age 40. **After conservative treatment of complicated appendicitis at age 40 or older**, there are now overlapping reasons to look for neoplasms in both the appendix (IA) and the colon (endoscopy).

Perioperative care after the operation is also being optimized:
- In non-perforated gangrenous appendicitis, a single antibiotic dose may be as good as continued postoperative therapy (Ozen 2026, antibiotic stewardship).
- In pediatric perforated appendicitis, postoperative intra-abdominal abscess remains the main complication (Borca 2026).
- An RCT of a modified ERAS protocol shortened length of stay (Calderón-Alvarado 2026).
- **Evidence-based nursing care** for pain management, infection prevention, and discharge support has joined this list (Stansell 2026).

For postoperative antibiotic stewardship, **"route"** has joined "duration" (Ozen 2026) as a question. Abdirahman Mohamud 2026 (NCT04803422) is a pragmatic, open-label, cluster-randomized crossover non-inferiority trial at 12 Danish hospitals. It enrolled 1021 adults with complicated appendicitis who had laparoscopic appendectomy (567 oral, 454 IV). **Three days of oral antibiotics was non-inferior to IV for 30-day intra-abdominal abscess**:
- ITT: 6.8% vs 4.9%, absolute difference 1.9% (90%CI −0.4 to 4.2), non-inferiority margin 5 points
- Per-protocol: 4.9% vs 3.0%

The oral group had **a 41.1-hour shorter hospital stay**, but **readmission was 4.7% higher** (95%CI 0.5-8.9). The authors recommend oral therapy as a routine option for clinically stable patients. However, the abscess rate was consistently a little higher with oral therapy. The trade-off "patients go home sooner, but slightly more come back" is worth including in the numbers given to patients. The abstract does not report which drugs were used or why patients were readmitted (abscess or other reasons).

Before asking how short and by which route antibiotics should be given, there is the question of what they should target. Almeida-Gomes 2026 is a systematic review of causative organisms in pediatric complicated appendicitis (23 observational cohort studies over 12 years). **The most common organism was Escherichia coli**, followed by Streptococcus, Bacteroides, Pseudomonas, Enterococcus, and Klebsiella species. This restates, for pediatric complicated disease specifically, the basis for empirical therapy: cover Enterobacteriaceae and anaerobes, and consider Pseudomonas and Enterococcus as well. Related debates rely on this bacterial picture: whether a single dose is enough (Ozen 2026), whether oral therapy is enough (Abdirahman Mohamud 2026, in adults), and how to prevent postoperative abscess (Borca 2026). However, the authors rate **the certainty of the evidence as low**. Sampling, microbiological methods, and outcome reporting vary widely, which makes comparison between studies and generalization difficult. The abstract does not report susceptibility patterns either. So this should be read not as a finding that changes regimen choice directly, but as "a rough list of the organisms to target."

### Epidemiology and pathophysiology
Lifetime risk ranges from 6-7% (Vidarsdottir 2025) to 7-9% (Kleindienst 2026), depending on the source, and peaks at age 10-19. Region, diet, and socioeconomic status have long been suspected as contributors. A review now takes the dietary hypothesis down to the level of mechanism and **formulates it as a "nutrition-microbiome-genetic axis"** (Ryoo 2026). This model complements, rather than replaces, the luminal obstruction theory. Consistent with this picture, **uncomplicated and complicated appendicitis have clearly different cytokine profiles** (IL-6, HGF, and MCP-1 raised in complicated disease; Han 2026, prospective, 113 patients). This gives immunological support to the view that "complicated appendicitis is not simply a later stage of uncomplicated appendicitis but a different disease." That view is also the theoretical basis for NOM working in uncomplicated disease. Almeida-Gomes 2026 tested the microbiome axis at the level of "which bacteria are present" and found E. coli predominant in pediatric complicated cases. However, its pooled analysis **included only studies that reported patient-level prevalence by culture**. Culture shows only the bacteria that grow. It does not measure the changes in gut microbial community structure that Ryoo 2026 describes. The "microbiome" of the pathophysiological model and the "causative organisms" in clinical specimens have not yet been connected.

For **viruses**, which may act before or alongside bacteria, a framework for sorting the findings has now been proposed. Berikkhanov 2026 is a structured narrative synthesis of 82 sources. It points out that viral findings in appendicitis have been interpreted without separating four scenarios:
1. **direct infection** of appendiceal tissue
2. the **host response** to systemic infection
3. a different condition that looks like appendicitis (**clinical phenocopy**)
4. effects of the gut **virome**

When the scenarios are separated, direct viral injury has been documented only **in rare clinicopathological situations**, and tissue studies **tend to argue against** frequent direct adenoviral infection. It is plausible that systemic infection triggers lymphoid activation, edema, and early microvascular injury that lead to appendicitis, but this **has not been shown as a causal chain in humans**. Clinical studies document that some uncomplicated cases resolve without antibiotics (cf. Lin 2026), but **there is no evidence that they resolved because they were viral**. The authors treat viral prodrome as "a candidate research variable whose independent diagnostic and prognostic value is not established." They call for prospective studies that link the timeline of infection to local inflammation and outcomes. The paper produces no new data, and the abstract does not describe its search or quality-appraisal methods. Its value is **a map that separates observed findings from competing causal hypotheses**. Combined with the work on causative organisms, the "microbes" in appendicitis are now discussed at three levels: **bacteria that grow in culture, the structure of the microbial community, and viruses (including the virome)**. None of these has yet been linked over time to local inflammation. The two-disease hypothesis (Han 2026) suggests that "appendicitis that subsides on its own" belongs to the uncomplicated side, but the biology behind it is still unknown.

In children, the link between nutritional status and perforation risk has also been studied. **Obesity or BMI alone does not predict perforation, while underweight and low albumin/prealbumin are more consistently associated with complicated disease** (Borca 2026, 14 studies). The core clinical point is unchanged: appendicitis can progress to perforation and abscess, so prompt diagnosis determines prognosis. This matters most at the two ends of the age range. **Neonatal appendicitis is so rare that it is easily overlooked, and delay and perforation are common** (Kılıç 2026). At the other end, in middle and older age, appendicitis can be **the first sign of a neoplasm** (appendiceal neoplasm in 15.2% of IA cases at ≥40, Wu 2026; colorectal neoplasm, Spota 2026). In primary care, "most children with acute abdominal pain have benign, self-limiting conditions" (Hogervorst 2026 assumes a usual-care referral efficiency of 88%). The gap between the prevalence seen in hospital populations and in primary care has itself become a design variable for diagnostic strategy. Findings also change with the time from onset to presentation, which is why ∆AAS works (Lastunen 2026). This shows that appendicitis is not a single fixed picture but **a process that changes over time**. The same applies to who is affected: pregnancy changes how well inflammation-based scores perform (Tavakoli 2026). **How findings should be read depends on the patient as well.**

### Open questions
- **Recurrence, QOL, and satisfaction beyond 5 years** in the antibiotics-first group. Kahana 2026 covered the period beyond 2 years (failure 38.9%, median 33.6 months), but "failure" is defined differently across studies, and the severity of recurrence and the patient experience are still a black box
- A large RCT of antibiotics vs observation without antibiotics (raised by Lin 2026), which would re-examine what "antibiotics-first" should actually involve
- **The biology of spontaneous resolution and the role of viruses**. What is different about uncomplicated cases that resolve without antibiotics? Do viral prodromes or positive viral tests have independent diagnostic or prognostic value? Prospective studies linking the timeline of infection to local inflammation and outcomes are needed (framework proposed by Berikkhanov 2026)
- How robust the pediatric NOM recommendation is. Replication is needed that holds up once prediction intervals are taken into account (Nasri & Dziri 2026). It also needs to be reconciled with the ~40% long-term failure rate (Kahana 2026, 5 of 9 studies pediatric)
- External validation of the continuous risk model pARC in other populations. Does its advantage over fixed-point scores (Tchouala Tchakoute 2026) hold outside Europe and North America and in adults? Can it actually be used once built into the EHR? (In adults, repeated AAS scoring is addressing the same gap in a different way)
- **External validation of ∆AAS (observe and re-score)**. Open points: the optimal observation time; whether the thresholds hold up (Lastunen 2026 is a secondary analysis of a single trial, n=83, with thresholds derived from its own data); whether it works in children and pregnant women; and whether the change over time can be built into a continuous model like pARC
- **Pregnancy-specific AIR cutoffs**. With only 5 studies and I² 75.6%, the cutoff shifts specificity substantially (Tavakoli 2026). Prospective multicenter studies should set thresholds by gestational age and define how AIR fits into triage before MRI
- The role of ERAT (endoscopic retrograde appendicitis therapy) in children, which needs trials comparing it with surgery and antibiotics (raised by Hong 2026)
- **An age threshold for interval appendectomy**. IA cases show 15.2% appendiceal neoplasm at ≥40 vs 2.1% below (Wu 2026). However, the age analysis rests on only 3 studies, age cut-points vary, and the denominator includes only patients selected for IA. We still need the true prevalence across all conservatively treated patients, and risk estimates that combine age with imaging findings
- Colonoscopic follow-up after appendicitis at age 40 or older. Indications and timing need prospective validation (framework proposed by Spota 2026). Could this be combined with the IA decision (Wu 2026), since both use the same age threshold?
- **Where postoperative oral antibiotics should stop being used**. Three days oral is non-inferior to IV, but readmission is 4.7% higher (Abdirahman Mohamud 2026). Still untested: what drives the readmissions, whether the results apply to children and to severe cases such as generalized peritonitis, and whether oral can be combined with a short course (single dose to 3 days, cf. Ozen 2026)
- Predictors of failure beyond the appendicolith, such as preoperative identification of complicated disease by combining biomarkers like IL-6 (+HGF/MCP-1), imaging, and AI
- Standardized research on causative organisms: multicenter studies using the same sampling and culture/sequencing methods (called for by Almeida-Gomes 2026). Can this, combined with susceptibility data, provide an evidence base for empirical regimens in pediatric complicated disease? Can the organisms found in clinical specimens be linked to changes in the gut microbiota (Ryoo 2026) and the virome (Berikkhanov 2026)?
- Multicenter, prospective external validation and implementation of AI diagnostic models (EHR/PACS integration). Can studies report not only discrimination (AUC) but also **calibration, clinical utility (decision curves), fairness, and reproducibility** in line with TRIPOD+AI / PROBAST+AI? (Bašković & Pogorelić 2026 offer a checklist and a research agenda.) There is also no established yardstick yet for evaluating large language models used for clinical decision support
- Whether a primary-care prediction rule + CRP POCT actually reduces over-referral without increasing delayed diagnosis. This awaits the results of Hogervorst 2026 (NCT06762275). Whether the approach transfers to free-access health systems without GP gatekeeping (including Japan) has not been examined
